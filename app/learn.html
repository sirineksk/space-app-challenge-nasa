Why the south pole is tricky

Four ideas, then a real example from our data.

The Sun always stays low

The Moon's axis is tilted only 1.5° from the Sun's path, so at the pole the Sun slowly circles the horizon instead of rising high. Even a 100 m hill casts a shadow almost 4 km long, which is why some crater floors never see sunlight at all.

shadow length L = h / tan(e) — at e = 1.5°, L ≈ 38·h. One full circle of the Sun takes a synodic month (29.5 days).

Earth bobs up and down

The Moon always shows the same face to Earth, so Earth barely moves in the lunar sky. But the Moon wobbles a little (libration), so Earth rises and sinks by about ±7° over 27 days. At the pole Earth sits right on the horizon: for about two weeks it is up, then two weeks it is hidden.

Libration in latitude ±6.7° (draconic month, 27.2 d). Earth's disk is ~1.9° wide seen from the Moon.

The Moon's curve hides far mountains

The Moon is small (radius 1,737 km), so the ground curves away fast. A mountain 50 km away appears about 720 m lower than it really is. Our skylines therefore read the terrain up to 300 km away in 720 directions, and account for that curve exactly.

drop ≈ d² / 2R. Horizon angle = atan2((R+hₜ)cos γ − (R+hₛ), (R+hₜ)sin γ), γ = d/R.

No air: no twilight, black shadows

Without an atmosphere there is no dusk glow and shadows are pitch black. Light depends only on how much of the Sun's disk clears the ridge. Because the Sun moves so slowly, it can take hours to slide behind a hill. We call a spot sunlit when at least half of the disk is visible.

visible fraction f = 1 − (acos x − x√(1−x²))/π, x = margin / disk radius. Power = 0.3 × 1361 W/m² × (1 AU/d)² × cos e × f.
